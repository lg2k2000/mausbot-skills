# Buying Process, Enterprise Realities and the Business Case (Selling With)

Added in the full-read revision. Load when designing deal stages, a business case, or a narrative with a champion. Quotes are verbatim from the book; everything else is paraphrase.

## Life inside the enterprise

- **Artificial drama.** Dee Hock: "in companies like this, procedure is more important than purpose, and method more important than results."
- **Handshake overhead** (Seth Godin): n(n-1)/2 connections; "10 people need 45 handshakes." Ask: "Do I know how many people are truly involved in approving this deal?" "What percent of those 45-plus handshakes have I influenced?" "What handshakes still need to happen before closing?"
- **Endless meetings are a feature.** If you don't know where you stand, your buyers likely don't either.
- **Extreme allergy to failure.** "A $10 million loss that’s caused by someone’s (failed) action is far, far worse than a $10 million loss that’s created by the way it’s always been done." Long-tenured employees can be strong allies.
- **Titles give safety**; the same idea is "outlandish" from an analyst and "visionary" from an SVP (Neil Soni).
- **Law of large numbers.** Size the problem against the account's scale.
- **A priority vs priorities.** "Priorities (plural) are mental filters." "A priority (singular) is something specific that executives are already sold on and actively subordinating other important topics to." "issues only become problems when they’re standing in the way of a priority."
- **The meeting, in the meeting, after the meeting.** Test: "I was thinking it’d be helpful to get the group’s input; would it be worth calling a meeting for everyone to weigh in?" A hedge means you're not the meeting.
- **Project risk before product risk.** "In a complex sale, the first risk is that no project is prioritized. So your product is never even evaluated."

## Seven buyer-behavior stages and exit criteria

"The steps you take to sell your product are not the steps your prospects take to buy it." Attach buying behaviors to the stages you already have.

1. **Frame a high-cost, high-priority problem in writing.** Exit: the buyer shares a written problem statement with their team.
2. **Multithreaded validation.** Exit: at least three buyers have read the problem statement, agreed with it, and tied it to an executive-level metric.
3. **An executive sponsors a new project.** Exit: the exec tasks the team with evaluating and recommending a course of action (bonus: the project gets a name).
4. **The committee agrees on an approach** (build, buy or partner). Exit: decision to buy, with requirements aligned to you.
5. **You're confirmed as provider of choice with a specific scope.** "You never want to negotiate terms until you’re confirmed as the provider of choice." Exit: other discussions stopped; everyone's input in the SOW.
6. **A compelling event drives the close date.** Exit: the buying team sets a target onboarding date and agrees to backdated tasks.
7. **Pricing, procurement and commercial terms finalized.** Exit: signed acceptance of all terms. The customer journey is just beginning.

**Bias-aware design** (aim for a low-blame decision): make buying collaborative, not concierge (IKEA effect); anecdotes before data (salience); champions repeat the problem statement every meeting (frequency illusion); buyers set pilot goals (expectation bias); deliver short-term wins during long cycles; "Assign blame to projects and fame to people."; ask "how" questions of novices and co-write for experts.

## The business case is the sales process

"Building a business case is the sales process." "Reps start drafting the business case after their very first call." Expected strength by stage: 2/5, 3/5, 4/5, 4.5/5, 4.9/5. A leader can inspect discovery, demos, internal alignment, multithreading, forecasting and champion building from one page.

Sales Velocity = (Close Rate x Average Contract Value x Number of Opportunities) / Sales Cycle Time.

### One-page business case template

- Title, and "Developed by: [Champion Name], [Key Deal Players]"
- Headline: "[Buying team or business unit] should [recommended action] by [timeline]. This will result in [outcome] while avoiding [cost of the problem] created by [problem]."
- The problem statement: who is impacted, the cost, the company-wide goal at risk, why it's getting worse.
- Recommended approach: vendor-agnostic first ("What must be true about how we go about solving this issue?"); "The approach is never “buy XYZ software.”"
- A payoff that matters: before vs after for daily users, and time to impact on exec metrics.
- Required investment: "Who needs to invest what, by when?" Offer two scenarios.

### The Build Loop (with the champion)

1. Start with a framework in mind.
2. Start with a few sentences in a shared space: "I’ll plan to build a short recap from what I’m understanding about the challenges we’re focused on, before our next call. Would you be open to sharing a few comments in between now and then?"
3. Expand the circle: "Who do you typically turn to for advice when a project is important to you? Do you think we could get their input too?" Share in edit mode: "What resonates, that you think we should highlight? And what do you think we’ve missed?"
4. Assign action items to fill gaps.
5. Socialize and gather co-authors: "A petition for change is only as strong as the number of signatures it gathers."
6. Combine feedback and refine. "You can’t write a “good” narrative by yourself."
7. Watch momentum: "Is my champion increasing the time they spend with me? Are they getting back to me in shorter time intervals?"

### Language that travels

- **Coordinating language:** reuse the buyer's exact words and structures.
- **Changing language:** a new mental container; be different, not better.
- **Elevating language:** name a villain instead of fighting feature wars. "What’s the dream life your customer wants to live?" "What obstacles stand in the way that you can help them overcome?"

## Discovery punch list (exact)

- Problem: "Has something changed inside your business that’s making this the priority now?" "When did you first notice X became a problem? What was happening then?" "Forgive me, but to make sure I’m understanding, why is this a problem?"
- Process: "Has your team set up a specific initiative to start solving this?" "Who from your team would share the most meaningful feedback with us?" "When was the last time your team evaluated a solution like this?"
- Payoff: "How would solving this show up in the rest of your business?" "How could this impact your own role or goals?" "Could you help me understand how any short-term goals for this might differ from your long-term goals?"
