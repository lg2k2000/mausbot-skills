# How to say no: the book's defenses, as audit criteria

Each principle chapter in *Influence* ends with a defense for the target. In this skill they serve two jobs:

1. **Buyer-side red flags** in Mode B: if a careful buyer would apply this defense to one of our moves, the move costs trust.
2. **Liam's own shield** when a vendor, partner, distributor or procurement team uses the principle on him.

Paraphrased unless quoted; quotes checked against the extracted text.

## Reciprocation

- The real opponent is the rule, not the requester. Rejecting every gift is a bad policy; it would cut you off from honest generosity.
- Accept favors for what they are. If one turns out to be a compliance device, redefine it as a trick, and the obligation disappears: "favors are to be met with favors; it does not require that tricks be met with favors."
- The same applies to concessions. A retreat from an inflated first ask is a tactic, not a gift.
- **Red flag for us:** a free assessment, lunch or event followed immediately by a hard ask; a concession the buyer can tell was planned; a first quote so high our retreat looks staged.

## Commitment and consistency

- Two signals. The **stomach**: a sense of being trapped into complying because of an earlier small yes. Response: tell the requester exactly what they are doing. The **heart of hearts**: "Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?" Trust the first flash of feeling.
- Emerson's reminder that blind consistency is foolish.
- **Red flag for us:** quoting a buyer's prior small yes as leverage; changing terms after agreement (lowball); pushing a public commitment the buyer did not choose; momentum built from tiny agreements toward a decision they never actually made.

## Social proof

- Two cases where the shortcut fails: **counterfeit evidence** (claques, canned laughter, staged testimonials presented as unrehearsed) and **innocent herd error** (everyone copying a mistake). Response to the first: notice, refuse and counterattack. Response to the second: periodically look up and check the crowd against objective facts and your own judgment.
- **Red flag for us:** customer claims with no named, comparable example; logo walls without permission or relevance; references chosen to look typical when they are not.

## Liking

- Don't try to block the causes of liking (attractiveness, similarity, compliments, contact, association); there are too many. Watch the effect: notice when you like the requester more than you would expect in the time you've known them. Then separate the person from the deal and judge the offer on its merits.
- **Red flag for us:** flattery heavier than the substance; instant claims of being alike; hospitality that outweighs the business content; a rep who seems to be fighting their own company for the buyer.

## Authority

- Remove the element of surprise. Ask "Is this authority truly an expert?" (real credentials, relevant to this question) and "How truthful can we expect the expert to be here?" (what do they gain).
- Beware the expert who admits a small drawback to buy credibility for a large claim.
- **Red flag for us:** a title introduced in place of relevant experience; a specialist answering outside their field; a token weakness admitted right before an overreach.

## Scarcity

- Scarcity raises arousal and arousal crowds out thinking. Step 1: treat the rush as a signal to stop and calm down. Step 2: ask why you want the item. If to own it, scarcity can be a fair guide to price. If to use it, remember scarce things don't work better: "the scarce cookies didn't taste any better".
- An endnote adds the key test: is the scarcity naturally occurring and honest, or fabricated?
- **Red flag for us:** an end date with no reason; claims of short supply; hints at other buyers; a meeting that ends on urgency rather than fit.
