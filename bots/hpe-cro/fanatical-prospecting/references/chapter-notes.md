# Fanatical Prospecting: chapter notes

Working notes from a full read of *Fanatical Prospecting* by Jeb Blount (Wiley, 2015), chapters 1-23 and the Notes. Paraphrased unless in quotation marks; quoted text was checked against the extracted book text. Figures are as the book reports them. Ethics flags mark material this skill excludes.

## Ch1 The case for prospecting

- Top performers share one trait: they prospect relentlessly. The Paradox of Basics: the secret is simple, not easy. The mantra: "One more call."
- The number one reason for failure in sales is an empty pipeline; the root cause is a failure to prospect. There is no easy button. You control your actions, your reactions and your mindset.

## Ch2 Seven mindsets

Optimistic and enthusiastic; competitive; confident; relentless; thirsty for knowledge; systematic and efficient (protect the Golden Hours, block time); adaptive and flexible (adopt, adapt, adept).

## Ch3 To cold call or not

- The real issue is willingness to interrupt, not whether a call is cold, warm or hot. The biggest prospects rarely come in through inbound; you have to interrupt them.
- A team calling dormant customers found buyers on a small number of dials. Reps fear making the call, not specifically the cold call. The question is how to balance channels.

## Ch4 Balanced prospecting

- Claiming to be "so much better" at one channel (usually in person) is often an excuse to avoid the phone. Diversify like an investor: phone, in person, email, social, text, referrals, networking, inbound, trade shows. The right mix depends on industry, product, deal complexity, customer base, tenure and geography. Don't copy an enterprise rep's regimen if you sell to small businesses, or the reverse.

## Ch5 The more you prospect, the luckier you get

- Universal Law of Need: the more you need a deal, the less likely you are to get it.
- The 30-Day Rule: "the prospecting you do in this 30-day period will pay off for the next 90 days." Transactional sales have a shorter version.
- Law of Replacement: prospects leave the pipe at your closing ratio, so replace at that rate.
- Anatomy of a slump: stop prospecting, the pipe empties, desperation sets in, closing rates fall. When in a slump, start prospecting; recovery takes about 30 days of activity. Consistency beats technique.

## Ch6 Know your numbers

- Track dials, contacts, emails, responses, appointments, sales, social touches and texts. Efficiency plus effectiveness equals performance. A rep who believed he made 50 calls had made 12: "You cannot be delusional and successful at the same time." Top performers track by hand.

## Ch7 The three Ps

- Procrastination, perfectionism, paralysis from analysis. A rep who over-researched made a handful of calls in hours; a peer who just dialed produced far more appointments and income. Research complex or C-level targets ahead of time, outside the Golden Hours. Fix: one call at a time.

## Ch8 Time

- Golden Hours are prime selling time; Platinum Hours (before and after) are for list building, research, precall planning, proposals, social, email prospecting and admin.
- Adopt a CEO mindset. Protect the Golden Hours: say no, prioritize, move non-sales work outside them, delegate.
- Time blocking and Horstman's Corollary to Parkinson's Law (work contracts to fit the time allowed). Inside reps made large daily call counts in three Power Hours with lists prepared in advance. "Anybody can stay focused for an hour."
- Concentrate: multitasking is a myth; one insurance team went from a few dials an hour to dozens by preparing lists, putting devices away and turning email off. Don't check email first thing; block the first one to two hours for the phone.
- Measure your worth: annual income goal divided by working Golden Hours gives an hourly value; don't spend it on low-value tasks.

## Ch9 The four objectives

- Set an appointment; gather information and qualify; close a sale; build familiarity.
- Complex, high-risk, high-cost sales: appointment with a qualified stakeholder first. Products sold on contract or budget windows: qualify the buying window first. New territory: gather information first.
- Prospecting is a contact sport: no chit-chat, no long scripts written by people who never prospected.
- An appointment is on both calendars with a set time and place. A rep whose "appointments" were vague had most of them no-show.
- Define the strike zone (decision roles, account size, buying and budget windows, contracts, trigger events). "Don't swing at nothin' ugly."
- Touches to engage, as the book estimates: 1-3 for an inactive customer; up to 20-50 for a cold prospect. Strategic prospecting campaigns run across channels.

## Ch10 The prospecting pyramid

- Treat the database as a pyramid. Bottom to top: little information; better information; buying windows identified (nurture); conquest prospects (your best 10-100); hot inbound leads and referrals; qualified prospects moving into the buying window (top of the daily list).
- Build lists with filters (objective, channel, qualification, potential, probability, role, vertical, trigger). Start at the top for early wins. Ask whether this is the best prospect to call.

## Ch11 Own your database

- The CRM is the most important tool; it remembers and reminds. Put every detail of every interaction in it. Small nuggets of information add up like a jigsaw.

## Ch12 The Law of Familiarity

- The more familiar a prospect is with you, the more likely they are to engage. Five levers: persistent and consistent prospecting; referrals and introductions (two steps: give a legendary customer experience, then ask); networking (not to sell, to connect; follow up within 24 hours); company and brand familiarity; personal branding (speak in public). Don't spend all your time building familiarity.

## Ch13 Social selling

- Five objectives: personal branding and familiarity; inbound via education and insight; trigger-event and buying-cycle awareness; research; outbound through direct engagement. Five Cs: connecting, content creation, content curation, conversion, consistency.
- Social alone is not enough; phone and email produce far more contact and conversion. In the book's challenge, a social-only rep sold nothing in a week while blended outreach produced deals. Social selling is not selling: don't pitch in feeds.
- For enterprise, social is a strategic game to influence key stakeholders. Use two to four channels; LinkedIn is primary for B2B. Profile: would you buy you? Think before you post.
- Strategic prospecting campaigns target a handful of conquest accounts: map decision makers and influencers, find coaches, build familiarity, give value first, be invited in when the window opens. Trigger events are disruptions in the status quo that open buying windows.
- Send a LinkedIn request every time you meet a prospect. Block 30-60 minutes daily, outside the Golden Hours.

## Ch14 Message matters

- Asking for time is the hardest request in the sales process. Avoid "I would love to have a few minutes of your time to tell you about my company", feature dumps and number-one claims. "Prospects meet with you for their reasons, not yours."
- An interrupted person wants it quick, transparent and relevant. Lower the risk (a short discovery call, not an hour-long demo).
- The power of because: the book cites the Langer copier study reported by Cialdini, and says not to use nonsense reasons.
- Targeted bridges (inferred problems of a group) vs strategic bridges (researched, for one person; for C-level and conquest). Use their language.
- "People make decisions based on emotion first and then justify with logic." Value types: emotional, insight, tangible. Test: what would make the prospect say "so what?"
- Asking: ask with confidence and assume you will get it; shut up; be ready for RBOs. Assertive asks win far more often (the book cites about 70% vs 30%). Rule of thirds: get to yes, no or maybe fast.

## Ch15 Telephone

- In one insurance bootcamp, agents made over a thousand dials with about half reaching someone. Contact rates vary widely by industry. The phone remains the most powerful prospecting tool; an outside rep makes 10-20 face-to-face touches a day vs 25-50 calls an hour.
- Reps find the phone awkward because of bad scripts, no process, RBO fear and rejection fear. Schedule a daily phone block; chunk dials.
- Five-step framework: name ("Hi, Julie."); identify yourself; "The reason I'm calling is..."; bridge with a because; ask and shut up. "The moment you pause you lose control of the call." Don't ask "How are you doing?" Avoid talk, tell, show. Match the ask to the objective: information, day and time, or an open question.
- Voicemail is for high-value prospects (conquest or near a window). Five steps: identify yourself; number twice; reason; reason to call back; name and number twice. Keep to 30 seconds.
- Timing calls is mostly an excuse. Eat the frog: block the first two hours for the phone.

## Ch16 Turning around RBOs

- Reflex responses are automatic ("We're not interested", "I'm busy"); brush-offs avoid conflict ("Call me later", send me information); objections are real and come with a because (a new contract, a locked budget).
- RBOs are finite: not interested, no budget, too busy, send information, overwhelmed, just looking. Write and rehearse turnarounds.
- "You cannot argue another person into believing that they are wrong." Three steps: Anchor, Disrupt, Ask. Disrupters: "Awesome. If you're happy, you shouldn't even think about changing!"; "I figured you would be."; "Tell me specifically what you are looking for."; "That makes sense. Most people aren't." Avoid "I understand". About half the time the ask draws a second RBO closer to the truth; after two, move on and come back another day.
- When the horse is dead, dismount. Ethics flag: the book also describes calling again after being told never to call. Excluded; respect opt-outs.

## Ch17 Gatekeepers

- Seven keys: be likable; please, please; be transparent; connect; hold the cheese (no tricks); ask for help; change the game (early or late, LinkedIn, in person, email, handwritten note).
- Hacks: the salespeople-help-salespeople approach is honest and usable. Ethics flag: the calling-other-extensions hack includes a misleading line to reception, and the go-around-back hack depends on setting; both are excluded for enterprise use.

## Ch18 In person

- A rep plans in-person touches around set appointments: 3-5 nearby prospects per appointment, researched, routed. Ethics flag: the same story includes walking in a back door and acting lost; excluded.
- In-person prospecting is the least efficient channel and best for local small-business sales; at large sites it mainly gathers information. Use it to supplement.
- Five-step hub-and-spoke system; five planning steps (research, personalize, objective, be ready to close, log); five-step call process (approach with confidence; identify yourself and say why; gather information; ask; turn around objections). Put your sales goggles on. "Awareness without action is useless."

## Ch19 Email

- Three cardinal rules: delivered, opened, converted.
- Delivered: one to one, no images, at most one full link including the signature, no attachments, no spam words, don't hit many people at one company at once, scrub bounces.
- Opened: familiarity (voicemail, LinkedIn and email together); subject lines 3-6 words, no questions, specific.
- Converted: AMMO plan (audience, method, message, outcome). Four elements: Hook, Relate, Bridge, Ask. No "Hi" or "Dear" before the name. The book's model email to a bank COO relates through other COOs rather than claiming to be one, and closes with a day-and-time ask. (This skill swaps that close for a booking link on cold email and a when-works question on warm email.)
- Write and schedule outside the Golden Hours. Proofread twice before sending.

## Ch20 Text

- Text is risky and intrusive without familiarity. The book cites Lead360 data: text alone converted few leads, but after phone contact text lifted conversion by over 100%.
- Networking follow-up: say you will text, send a LinkedIn request, text a thank-you and meeting request within 24 hours, retry once, then switch channels.
- Use for trigger events, nurturing with useful links, and congratulations. Seven rules: identify yourself; message matters; direct and brief (under 250 characters); no abbreviations; transparent links; pause and reread; know your numbers.

## Ch21 Mental toughness

- Four pillars: desire (what do you want, how will you get it, how bad do you want it); mental resilience (keep learning); physical resilience (exercise, sleep, food); feed your attitude. When in first place, attack yourself.

## Ch22-23

- "When it is time to go home, make one more call." The only question that matters: "How bad do you want it?"
