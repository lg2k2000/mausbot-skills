# Influence

## What this skill does

The single home for Robert Cialdini's *Influence* in Liam's sales work: a field manual plus a Coach mode and an Audit mode. Cialdini spent years inside sales, fundraising and advertising operations and found that most compliance tactics fall into six categories, each powered by one psychological principle: **reciprocation, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, and scarcity**. Material self-interest (a good deal) is real but treated in the book as a given, not a principle.

The core idea: people use mental shortcuts, which Cialdini calls "click, whirr" responses, because modern life is too busy to analyze every decision. The shortcuts are usually right. They become dangerous when someone fakes the cue that triggers them.

This skill uses the principles in one direction only: to make a true, relevant message easier for a busy IT buyer to act on. It never manufactures the cue.

It does not run discovery or qualify deals. `gap-selling` still owns the problem, its impact and the change cost; `sandler-selling` still owns qualification and agreements. This skill shapes how a true point is framed, sequenced and asked.

# Ethics (do not violate)

Liam is a seller and the buyer knows it. Cialdini draws the line himself: practitioners who use the principles on real evidence are fair partners; the enemy is anyone who will "falsify, counterfeit, or misrepresent" the evidence that cues the shortcut. We stay on the fair side.

**Required:**

- Every proof point is real, current and checkable: named customer stories only with permission, numbers with a source, peer examples that are truly similar.
- Every deadline or constraint is real: an actual price-hold expiry, a real quarter-end program date, a real allocation or lead-time issue, a real renewal date the buyer owns.
- Every gift (an assessment, a workshop, a benchmark, an intro) is useful on its own and carries no hidden string. If the buyer takes it and buys nothing, that is fine.
- Commitments we ask for are ones the buyer would stand behind later, knowing exactly why we asked.
- Authority comes from real expertise (HPE specialists, architects, published results), introduced for its relevance, never for its title alone.

**Forbidden:**

- Manufactured scarcity: invented last units, fake end dates, phantom competing buyers, a claim that a manager will only approve the price today
- Fake or inflated social proof: "many customers" that do not exist, borrowed logos, cherry-picked references presented as typical, staged testimonials
- Pressure: rushing a buyer past their own process, exploding offers, guilt over a gift, using an earlier small yes as a trap
- The lowball: agreeing a price, scope or term, then removing it once the buyer is committed
- Rejection-then-retreat with a first ask we knew was absurd, or a staged fight with our own manager on the buyer's behalf
- Flattery we do not mean, mirroring that is an act, or similarity we invent
- Exploiting a buyer's bad news, fear or internal politics to create urgency

**The test:** if the move would still work when the buyer knew exactly what we were doing and why, it is in bounds. If it only works because they would feel tricked later, drop it.

# How it plugs into other skills

**`gap-selling`.** Urgency comes from a quantified gap, never from a scarcity frame. If there is no measured impact, this skill does not supply a deadline to cover the hole. Use commitment and consistency to help the buyer state their own current state and impact in their words; Gap Selling decides whether the gap is real.

**`selling-with`.** Liking and social proof are how a champion carries the story inside. Give the champion true peer examples and the buyer's own stated goals (consistency) to repeat. Never ask a champion to overstate.

**`sandler-selling`.** The up-front contract is a fair, active, public commitment. Keep it. Sandler's permission to say no is the honest counterweight to every principle here: it removes pressure.

**`jolt`.** When a buyer is stuck in indecision, more scarcity or more social proof backfires. JOLT owns that phase; from this skill use only true peer stories and real (not invented) limits on the offer.

**`elicitation`.** Elicitation owns how questions are asked. Reciprocation (share something first), liking (genuine common ground) and authority (credible peer examples) make those prompts work. Same ethics test.

**`fanatical-prospecting`.** The "because" research that book cites is the Langer study Cialdini reports. Prospecting asks for time; this skill makes the reason honest and the proof real.

# Mode A: Coach

Use for framing an ask, a follow-up, a proof point, a meeting close, or a reply to a stall.

**Output:**

- 4-8 speakable lines or email sentences, each labeled in parentheses with the principle and, where useful, the Gap Selling element it supports
- For each line, one sentence on why it is honest (what real fact it rests on)
- 2-4 coaching notes: which principle fits this buyer now, what to leave out, where a line could tip into pressure
- 1 clear next step

**Rules:**

- Start from facts we actually have. If a proof point, date or peer example is missing, say so and write the line with a bracketed placeholder instead of inventing one.
- Prefer one principle per line. Stacked principles read as a pitch.
- Default order for a first meeting: liking and reciprocation (earn the conversation), then authority and social proof (why us, shown not claimed), then commitment (a small, real next step). Scarcity only when a real constraint exists, stated plainly.
- Give a reason with every ask. A plain "because" is honest and works; a fake reason is forbidden.
- For existing customers, consistency with their own stated goals beats any new frame.

**Example lines (fit to the deal before use):**

- (Reciprocation) "Before we talk products, here is the capacity snapshot our architect pulled from your current estate. Keep it whether or not we work together."
- (Commitment, small and active) "Would you be willing to write down the two outcomes this project has to hit? I will build the next session around exactly those."
- (Social proof, similar peer) "A [sector] team about your size moved [workload] last year. The part that surprised them was [true detail]. Happy to set up a call with them if useful."
- (Authority, relevant and candid) "Our storage architect has done this migration [N] times. She will also tell you where it is not a fit."
- (Liking, real common ground) "You mentioned you came up through the network team. So did our lead engineer, which is why he scoped this the way he did."
- (Scarcity, only if true) "The current price hold ends on [real date]. If the decision lands after that, I will re-quote, no drama either way."

# Mode B: Audit (strict)

Use when Liam provides a transcript, call notes, an email, or a sequence and asks for critique.

**Required output (use this exact structure):**

1. **What actually happened (facts only)**: who asked for what, what was agreed
2. **Moves used**: principle, a short quote, and whether it was honest and effective
3. **Moves missed**: where a principle would have helped (no reason given for an ask, no peer proof offered, no small commitment secured, a gift given with no follow-up), with a better line
4. **Moves misused**: any line that fails the ethics test (manufactured scarcity, vague or fake social proof, pressure, title-dropping, lowball, extreme first ask), quoted, with a corrected version
5. **Buyer-side red flags**: places where a careful buyer would apply Cialdini's own defenses against us (table below). Each one costs trust even if the deal moves.
6. **Best next lines** (5-8 max)

Read full transcripts, never summaries. If speaker labels are unreliable, attribute by context and say so.

### Buyer-side red flags (the book's "how to say no" defenses)

Each principle chapter ends with how a target protects themselves. In an audit, check whether any of our moves would trip one. If it would, the move is a liability.

| Principle | The buyer's defense in the book | Red flag in our call or email |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciprocation | Accept favors for what they are; if a gift turns out to be a compliance device, redefine it as a trick. In the book's words: "favors are to be met with favors; it does not require that tricks be met with favors." | A free assessment or event followed at once by a hard ask; a concession that looks staged; a retreat from a first ask we never expected to get |
| Commitment and consistency | Two signals. The stomach: a sense of being trapped into complying, so tell the requester what they are doing. The heart: "Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?" | Quoting the buyer's earlier small yes back as a lever; a changed price, scope or term after agreement; pushing for a public commitment they did not choose |
| Social proof | Watch for counterfeit evidence (staged testimonials, fake crowds) and for innocent herd errors; check the claim against objective facts | Customer claims with no names; references that are not truly similar; a logo slide the buyer can't verify |
| Liking | Notice liking the seller more than expected in a short time, then separate the seller from the deal and judge the deal on its merits | Heavy flattery, rapid mirroring, gifts or hospitality that outweigh the substance of the meeting |
| Authority | Ask "Is this authority truly an expert?" and "How truthful can we expect the expert to be here?" Beware a small admitted drawback used to buy credibility for a big claim | Titles introduced instead of relevant experience; a token weakness admitted to set up an overreach; a specialist speaking outside their field |
| Scarcity | Treat a rush of arousal as a signal to stop and calm down, then ask whether you want to own the thing or use it, since scarce things don't work any better | Deadlines with no stated reason, claims of short supply, competing-buyer hints, a meeting that ends on urgency instead of fit |

Also run this check the other way: if a vendor, partner or procurement team uses one of these moves on Liam, name the principle and the defense in one line.

**Audit rules:**

- Quote the actual words. Do not grade a move you cannot point to.
- A principle used on a true fact, clearly stated, is a strength. Call it out.
- Flag missing reasons on any ask for time, data or a decision.
- If a move fails the ethics test, the fix is to remove or replace it, not to make it subtler.

## I. Weapons of influence

- **Fixed-action patterns and trigger features.** Animals and people respond to a single trigger feature with a whole automatic sequence. Cialdini's shorthand for the automatic response is "click, whirr."
- **The power of a reason.** In Ellen Langer's copier study, a request with a reason ("because I'm in a rush") got far more compliance than one with none, and even an empty reason ("because I have to make some copies") nearly matched the real one. For small requests the word "because" does much of the work. For us: always give the real reason.
- **Expensive equals good.** When unsure of quality, people use price as a stand-in for value.
- **The contrast principle.** The second of two things is judged against the first: show the larger item first and the smaller seems smaller (the book's examples: suit before sweater, a deliberately poor setup house before the real one, car add-ons after the price is agreed). Honest use: when presenting options, show the full scope and the phased scope side by side so the buyer can see the real difference. Dishonest use: a decoy option nobody should buy.
- **Jujitsu.** Influence works by borrowing forces already in the target's head, so the practitioner looks like they did nothing. That is exactly why the ethics test matters.

## II. Reciprocation

**The rule:** we try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us. Every society runs on it.

- **The rule is overpowering.** In the Regan study, a small unrequested favor (a soft drink) roughly doubled later purchases from the giver, and it worked even when people did not like the giver.
- **The rule enforces uninvited debts.** A gift nobody asked for still creates an obligation (an appeal mailed with address labels nearly doubled its response). There is an obligation to give, to receive and to repay, and the obligation to receive is what makes it exploitable.
- **The rule can trigger unfair exchanges.** A small favor can draw a much larger return, because being in debt is uncomfortable.
- **Reciprocal concessions.** A concession invites a concession. The **rejection-then-retreat** technique: a larger request, refused, followed by a smaller one, gets far more yeses (in the zoo-trip study, compliance roughly tripled). Side effects: people who agree this way feel more responsible for the outcome, are more satisfied, and are more likely to follow through and agree to later requests. Limits: a first ask so extreme it is not seen as good faith backfires.
- **Honest B2B use:** lead with something useful (an assessment, a benchmark, a peer intro) with no strings. Offer a real full-scope option and a real phased option; if the buyer declines the full scope, moving to the phase is a real concession, not a staged one. After a no to a deal, a referral request is a legitimate smaller ask.
- **Don't:** inflate the first quote to have room to concede, or present a gift and then call in the debt.

## III. Commitment and consistency

**The rule:** once we make a choice or take a stand, we feel pressure to behave consistently with it. Consistency is usually valued and useful, and automatic consistency spares us from thinking.

- **Commitment is the key.** Getting a small, initial commitment changes how people see themselves. The **foot-in-the-door technique**: a small request first (a small sign) makes a large related one (a big billboard) far more likely to be accepted. Start small and build.
- **What makes a commitment stick:** it is **active** (written or done, not just thought), **public**, **effortful**, and most of all an **inner choice** the person owns rather than one bought with a big reward or forced by a threat. A buyer who writes the agreement in their own words keeps it better than one who signs ours.
- **Commitments grow their own legs.** People add new reasons to support a choice they already made, so the choice can survive the loss of the reason that started it. That is what the **lowball** exploits: an advantage is offered, the decision is made, then the advantage is removed.
- **Honest B2B use:** ask the buyer to state their own success criteria and write them into the mutual plan (with `selling-with`); confirm next steps in writing after each meeting; secure small real steps (a data pull, a workshop date, an architecture review) before a large one. Let the buyer own the choice.
- **Don't:** lowball, quote a buyer's small yes back as a trap, or make them commit in front of their boss to box them in.

## IV. Social proof

**The rule:** we decide what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct, especially what they do.

- **Strongest under uncertainty.** When a situation is unclear, people look to others, which produces **pluralistic ignorance** (everyone looks calm, so no one acts). The book's fix for emergencies: reduce uncertainty and single out one person ("You, sir, in the blue jacket, I need help").
- **Strongest among similar others.** People follow those like themselves (same age, role, situation) far more than generic crowds.
- **Honest B2B use:** a specific named peer in the same sector and size, with permission; a reference call; real adoption numbers with a source. In a buying committee where nobody wants to move first, name one owner and one next step (the blue-jacket move).
- **Don't:** claim everyone is moving, show vague logos, offer references that aren't similar, or present cherry-picked wins as typical.

## V. Liking

**The rule:** we prefer to say yes to people we know and like. The Tupperware party works because the hostess, a friend, is the real requester.

The factors in the book:

- **Physical attractiveness** (a halo effect people don't notice in themselves)
- **Similarity** (in background, interests, style; we underestimate its pull)
- **Compliments** (praise works even when we suspect it)
- **Contact and cooperation** (familiarity helps only when the contact is pleasant; working toward a shared goal builds liking)
- **Conditioning and association** (we link people to the news they bring; people also bask in reflected glory by associating with winners)
- **Honest B2B use:** real common ground, sincere and specific recognition of the buyer's work, framing the project as a joint effort against a shared problem, being the person who brings useful news.
- **Don't:** fake similarity, flatter, or stage a fight with our own manager on the buyer's behalf.

## VI. Authority

**The rule:** we have a deep-seated sense of duty to authority. Milgram's experiments showed ordinary adults going to extreme lengths on an authority's instruction.

- **Connotation, not content.** Symbols of authority work even without the substance: **titles, clothes, trappings**. People underestimate how much these sway them.
- **Honest B2B use:** bring the specialist whose experience is relevant, introduce them by what they have done, and let them say where the solution is not a fit. Real credibility includes candor about limits.
- **Don't:** lean on titles, impersonate expertise, or admit a trivial drawback as a setup for an inflated claim.

## VII. Scarcity

**The rule of the few:** opportunities seem more valuable when they are less available. Losses loom larger than gains.

- **Tactics in the book:** the **limited-number** tactic and the **deadline tactic**, used honestly or not.
- **Psychological reactance:** when a freedom is threatened, we want the thing more. Banning or restricting something can raise desire and even belief.
- **Exclusive information** persuades more. In the beef-importer study, scarcity news doubled orders and scarcity plus exclusive information multiplied them about sixfold; the author notes the information used was true.
- **Optimal conditions:** newly scarce beats always scarce, and scarcity caused by competition from others is strongest. That is why fake rival buyers work, and why we never use them.
- **Honest B2B use:** frame in terms of a real loss the buyer has already quantified (with `gap-selling`); state real dates (end of support, renewal, a price hold, a funding program window) plainly with the reason; share genuinely new information first with the buyer it affects.
- **Don't:** invent end dates, quantities or rivals, or let urgency replace fit.

## VIII. Instant influence

People rely on single-cue shortcuts more when they are rushed, stressed, uncertain, distracted or tired. Enterprise IT buyers are often all five. That raises our duty: when the shortcut is likely to fire, the cue must be true.

## IX. Anti-patterns

- Naming the principle in a customer email or slide
- Stacking principles in one message (gift, deadline, crowd and title together)
- Urgency without a quantified gap
- Customer claims with no named, similar peer
- A first quote padded to create a fake concession
- A concession that disappears after the buyer commits (lowball)
- Asks with no reason given
- Using liking to soften a weak offer instead of fixing the offer
- Treating a buyer's no as a debt they owe us

## X. Checklist

**Before:** What real fact supports each line? Which one principle fits this buyer now? What small, real commitment is the next step? Is any date, number or peer in the draft unverified?

**During:** Give a reason with each ask. Let the buyer own their commitments. If pressure starts to show, slow down.

**After:** Confirm agreements in writing in the buyer's words. Deliver anything promised. Run the ethics test on anything that felt slick and retire it.
