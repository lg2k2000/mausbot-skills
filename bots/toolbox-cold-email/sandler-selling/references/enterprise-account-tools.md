# Enterprise Account Tools (Mattson and Sullivan, Sandler Enterprise Selling)

The 13 SES tools, plus the other enterprise how-tos, in the order the book uses them. Field lists come from the book's tool figures (images in the epub, typed by eye). Double-quoted text is word for word. Everything else is paraphrase. Exact scripts and questions live in `book-scripts.md`.

## Stage 1: Territory and account planning

**Market understanding (Ch 1).** Six areas: account base, economy, service structure and deployment, market patterns, competition, and affiliations and alliances. For each one, pick the single topic with the biggest impact. Rule: "Know your market better than anyone."

**SWOT (Ch 2).** Strengths and weaknesses are internal. Opportunities and threats are external. Do one for the territory.

**KARE tags (Ch 3).** One tag per account:

- Keep: a current client you want to keep (the attribute grid says maintenance, low growth, low vulnerability).
- Attain: a new target that matches your profile.
- Recapture: a former client.
- Expand: a major current client with strong growth potential and high vulnerability.

"No account can be in two KARE categories—you will have to pick one." Hunters fit Attain and farmers fit Expand. Ending a relationship is a separate call.

**Territory value proposition (Ch 4).** What it is, what it does, how it helps the client, and how you measure it, all in 30 seconds or less. It feeds the 30-second commercial and, later, the deal-level value proposition. Guide: "Bring value or don't show up."

**Growth Account Booster (Ch 5).** Ten steps:

1. Intro.
2. Sales team: who does what.
3. Relationship snapshot: history, current data, competitive info.
4. Client cast of characters: traits, tendencies, action items.
5. Five-year revenue span, back and forward.
6. Current sales activity.
7. Account relevance: how deep and wide you are.
8. Top six opportunities.
9. Top six value propositions, as value arrows.
10. Top six advancement actions.

Rule: "Hunt in packs." Keep the plan current, not in a binder. Share everything inside the team.

**RACI (Ch 5).** Responsible does the work. Accountable is one person who owns the result and delegates. Consulted gives two-way counsel. Informed gets one-way updates.

## Stage 2: Opportunity identification

**Prospecting plan (Ch 6).** Set monthly contacts, conversations and touch points, by source. The weekly log covers phone calls; LinkedIn, email or other media; referral meetings; and networking events, each as goal against actual. The Success Triangle is Behavior (goals, plans, actions), Attitude (confidence, outlook, responsibility) and Technique (strategy, preparation, focus). Rule: "Feed the funnel."

**30-second commercial (Ch 7, Table 7.1).**

1. Introduction: name, company, type of business.
2. Capsule summary: a pain statement (the pain you fix) and a benefit statement (built from your value proposition).
3. Hook question: is this relevant to them?

"Once you pose the hook question, you must stop talking and wait for the other person to respond." To find pain statements, ask why clients buy this type of product, why they buy it from you, and what they give up if they don't.

**Value arrows (Ch 7, 18).** "When we have this [name the product/service] . . ." / ". . . we will be able to . . ." / ". . . which means that our organization will benefit by . . ." / ". . . and we'll measure the improvements through . . ."

**LinkedIn Levers (Ch 7).** A two-part checklist covering the person's profile and the company page. Before you put weeks into an RFP, check the decision maker's ties. In the book's story, a teammate found the COO had a decade-long tie to the competitor, and the team ended the pursuit.

**DISC (Ch 8).** Each style's secret fear, from the book's profiles: D, that someone will take advantage of them; I, rejection; S, loss of security; C, being forced to choose between quality and relationships. How to sell to each:

- D: "tell them the bottom line, give them a few choices, and then let them decide how to proceed."
- I: a "friendly, sociable approach in which they are not rushed or controlled".
- S: "Work in stages and respect the high S's relationships. The high S needs a steady pace."
- C: "Forget small talk and pleasantries."

High-I sellers tend to skip the post-sell terms. Ask them: how exactly will you do that?

**Wake-up call (Ch 8).** How many people in your top account would call you back over a weekend? Most sellers say zero. Script in `book-scripts.md`.

**Relationship Builder (Ch 8, Fig 8.4).** For each person in the cast: role, personal goals and wins, LinkedIn connection, DISC, and relationship (Friend, Neutral or Enemy). Then list three value enhancers (actions you can take now) and three long-term actions. The book says it calls for "brutal honesty and specific plans". If the actions are hard to write, you still have research to do.

**Up-front contract, five parts (Ch 9).**

1. Objective.
2. Time.
3. Prospect's agenda: what the buyer agrees, in advance, to prepare or bring.
4. Salesperson's agenda: what you plan to do, with the buyer's OK.
5. Outcome: a defined ending agreed in advance.

Use UFCs inside your own company too: between sales and delivery, between manager and rep, and on forecasts. Rule: "Agreements drive actions."

**Three Opportunity Planner (Ch 9, Fig 9.1).** List the three highest-probability deals likely to close within 60 days, with account, KARE tag, value, probability, close date and a value proposition for each. "Sooner and smaller is more accurate for income/cash-flow predictions than later, bigger and less certain." Manager and seller agree the short horizon, and the forecast "must be based on reality, not wishful thinking." Big accounts are "transaction engines": small deals open the door.

## Stage 3: Qualification

**Positioning tool (Ch 10, Fig 10.2).** Opportunity value, probability and close date. For each buyer: role, impact (High, Medium or Low), DISC, and relationship (Friend, Neutral or Enemy). Your solution in four value-arrow rows. For each competitor: offer, strength, areas to exploit, and their coaches. The big question is the competitor's value proposition for this buyer in this deal. Plan who inside the account can tell you what it is.

**Pre-call Planner (Ch 11, Fig 11.1).** Attendees, account, KARE tag. For each buyer: impact, met before?, LinkedIn connection, DISC, relationship. Prebrief relationship issues, business issues, and roles. Then list what to bring, the call's goals, key questions, the questions the buyer may ask with your answers, and the planned UFC. Match the team to the buyer's style. For a high-D buyer and a high-C rep, add someone who talks bottom line. "There are always two teams: the buyers and the sellers. Very often, the buying team has the upper hand."

**Call Debrief (Ch 11, Fig 11.2).** Contact, KARE tag, attendees, new information, planned next steps, red flags, topics for next time, and action steps (who, when, intended result). Do it in writing right after the call and send it to the team. "Write it all down!"

**Opportunity tool (Ch 12, Fig 12.3).** Account, KARE type, sales lead. For each competitor: their offer, your edge, and their coaches. For each pain: the business impact, budget found, and how much the org cares (aware, concern, critical). Any blank is "a red flag—a warning sign."

**Coaches (Ch 12).** Internal advocates, and "you will need more than one". Get them from the user group, the buying group (procurement, purchasing, supply chain), the technical group, and several influencers who are affected but don't buy. Influencers "can ignite an internal revolution toward, or away from, your product or service." Senior people are not always the best informed: "Sometimes, the highest levels of an organization can be the last and least informed." The exact coach ask is in `book-scripts.md`.

**Budget (Ch 13).** Willing and able to invest money, time, resources and political capital. "Willing minus able equals unqualified for budget." When they can pay but won't, look for two roadblocks: technical (will it work here?) and conceptual (does it fit their strategy?). Questions in `book-scripts.md`.

**Decision (Ch 14).** Who, what, how, where, when and why. The facts change over time, so stay engaged. If a competitor got in first, run a fresh go/no-go. Questions in `book-scripts.md`.

## Stage 4: Solution development

**Refine the position (Ch 15).** Review with the whole team before going further:

- the cast and their pains;
- the competition (footprint, history, value proposition);
- LinkedIn Levers and the Relationship Builder again;
- the top 10 business requirements.

The 10 requirements are the problem; the core of the new capability; the users and how they'll use it; how it addresses the pain; the benefits; when it's needed; dependencies; risks; what's in and out of scope; and key assumptions. "Double check all your assumptions!"

**Pursuit Navigator (Ch 16).** Go/no-go across functions (sales, legal, accounting, marketing, delivery, executives). List risks in three groups: client, selling team, and financing or contract. Mark each Stable or Needs mitigation, with an owner, a plan and a deadline. Book example client risks (Table 16.1):

- The client doesn't understand its own responsibilities.
- No relationships at more than one level.
- The true buyers haven't been in the talks.
- The client uses several vendors like you.
- The client doubts your solution.
- Losing here would hurt other business with the client.
- The client won't talk clearly and often.

A single point of contact is a major risk. The cost of staying in includes team time, opportunity cost, "the risk of providing free consulting", and leaking information to competitors. "Even one unmitigated risk means the deal is probably unwinnable or would result in a piece of business you might come to regret winning." "Sometimes getting out is a gift". If there are too many risks, ask the buyer to restructure the deal. If they won't, exit, usually telling them why, or go on with eyes open. "In the enterprise world, there is no halfway. If you are not 100 percent committed to a pursuit, others likely are." It's also the basis of the postmortem.

**Build and form the team (Ch 17).** Name a proposal manager early. Add internal people (legal, contracts, finance, IT, PM, marketing, service lines, executives) and outside people (subcontractors, writers, alliance and channel partners, experts). Outsiders must not compete in the account; get NDAs. Don't pick people just because they're free: "Hold out for the right team." Use RACI.

**Compelling themes and value links (Ch 18).** Build a value proposition for this deal and these buyers. Value links are "specific key words from the value proposition that you know resonates with this specific client". Examples: responsiveness, dependability, scalability, speed to market. Repeat them through the proposal and every talk track, and share them with the team.

**Fingerprinting (Ch 19).** Before you finalize, get feedback from the people who decide. "People are more likely to be persuaded by their own words than by yours." If procurement rules limit contact, follow them and get input through informal talks with supporters. Don't ask leading questions; you want the truth, even if it means you withdraw.

## Stage 5: Proposing and advancement

**Developing the response (Ch 20).** Eight parts: strategy review, business case, ROI, reference checks, pricing, final solution, draft, draft review. The sales lead stays accountable for all eight. Eight tips:

1. Keep client-centric guidance throughout.
2. Leave an empty chair for the client at planning meetings.
3. Pick the right people, not just the available ones.
4. Use fresh references.
5. Truly customize.
6. Fingerprint at every critical point.
7. Use a value proposition and value links that match the buyer's pains.
8. Use their industry's and their company's language.

Pre-present the highlights to your best contacts, informally. Rule: "Bid on the business you've earned the right to win."

**Monkey's paw (Ch 20).** "a little, easy-to-gain agreement that sets up a big partnership with the target organization." Types: proof of concept, prototype, pilot, trial, quick-start. Use one when the client's culture, a competitor's strategy, a need for quick results, a funding limit, or a need to verify your approach calls for it. "Successful execution typically means clear sailing to winning the overall bid, while failure usually means that you are done for good." Book story: one rep sold $50K studies under the buyer's signing limit, skipped committees, halved the cycle and extended 86% of them.

**Presentation (Ch 21).** First learn whether it comes with the proposal or after a down-select. Find out who will attend; new business users and executives often first appear here. Rehearsal is "the ultimate in pre-call planning". Refresh:

- the remaining competition;
- your value proposition;
- where each key buyer stands;
- the agenda, roles and logistics;
- your theme and strategy.

The lead presenter is the one "whose presence is most likely to secure the deal". Plan how to answer each competitor and their supporters. Copy the room and the tech. Have a bare-bones version ready.

Checklist highlights:

- Open with a UFC and reconfirm the time.
- Start with their needs and pains and get agreement.
- Don't read slides.
- Use action phrases: "We do this," not "We plan to do this."
- Check the room's temperature at key points.
- Have the right person answer each question.
- Get clear next steps.
- "Beat your time contract."

Self-check, yes or no:

- Did you identify every decision maker attending?
- Did you research each one on LinkedIn?
- Did you rehearse at least an hour?
- Did you preview the solution informally?
- Did you practice with colleagues, get critique, and include other functions?

"Even one ‘no’ answer is cause for concern."

Structure: opening UFC, set the stage (value proposition), introductions and roles, delivery with value links, transitions, recap, and close with set action items and next steps. A key executive opens with a short commitment. No one presents without a clear role. Within 24 hours the lead sends each buyer a personal email covering their action items and pains. No boilerplate.

**Selection (Ch 22).** Rule: "You win, or you learn." After a loss:

1. Thank the organization and each buyer.
2. Ask about subcontracting to the winner.
3. Ask for a debrief.

Debrief questions:

- Who won, and how many bid?
- Is scoring available?
- Was the winner cheapest? Where did your price rank?
- What were your strengths and weaknesses?
- Were you technically acceptable?
- How did you compare with the winner?
- What was the winner's edge, and what was yours?
- What was the main reason they won, and the main reason you didn't?
- What could you have done differently?
- What future work fits you?

Then run an internal postmortem with the Pursuit Navigator, even with no client debrief. Do one after wins too: in one book story, a win debrief found a VP had voted for the competitor, and fixing that grew the account.

**Agreement and transition (Ch 23).** Take the verbal yes to a fair, signed contract. "Celebrating early is a margin-killer." Plan the startup, but "defer any significant financial and personnel commitments until you have signed contracts." Before kickoff, sales and delivery check together:

- org chart and roles;
- scope, approach and goals;
- management approach and schedule;
- deliverables and quality plan;
- WBS and acceptance procedures;
- reviews, assumptions and constraints;
- risk mitigation and methodology.

## Stage 6: Service delivery

**Client-centric Satisfaction (Ch 24, Fig 24.1).** Sales and delivery meet the client together at the start, face to face or by voice. The client picks five of the book's common factors (such as quality of deliverables, meeting deadlines, communications, responsiveness and knowledge transfer) or adds their own. They weight each 1 to 5. After an agreed time, they rate each 1 to 5, and you score weight times rating. Revisit every three to six months. Rule: "Let the client define success." In the book's story, a client with passing survey grades canceled within 60 days over remote-site service the survey never asked about.

**Business requirements focus (Ch 25).** After the win, review the same 10 requirements with delivery. Keep checking the original pain, user engagement and the first risks.

**Team Storm (Ch 26, Fig 26.1).** A fast, low-tech team session on one known issue, with the right people. Five steps:

1. Problem statement: a "how to" headline.
2. Background.
3. Idea generation, fast, on flip charts.
4. Idea selection: vote for the top four.
5. Action plan: who does what by when.

A facilitator leads it.

**Client2 (Ch 27).** Treat each client as "a market unto itself". "There is no better time to win business than after you've won business." Five areas:

1. Organic growth: renewal, expansion, other client areas, other locations, your other products.
2. Partnerships and alliances: the client's partners as markets, joint selling and delivery.
3. Family tree: sister companies, subsidiaries, parents, business partners, trade groups.
4. Alumni: client and partner alumni at new firms, and referrals into former employers.
5. The customer's customer: a "connected benefit". Get the client's OK before any contact.

Daily question: "What have I done today to grow this client?"

**Client retention (Ch 28).** The 10 principles:

1. Do what you say you'll do.
2. Know your client and stay updated.
3. Communicate constantly.
4. Deliver value.
5. Listen inside and out.
6. Sell and deliver as a team.
7. Anticipate the next move that serves the client.
8. Be proactive.
9. Care.
10. Play the long game.

Principle: "Do what you say you'll do."
