# Influence: chapter notes

Working notes from a full read of *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (new edition, with Reader's Reports) by Robert B. Cialdini. Paraphrased unless in quotation marks; quoted text was checked against the extracted book text. Figures are as reported in the book. No page numbers are given because the source was an EPUB without stable pagination.

## Introduction

- Cialdini studied compliance professionals from the inside (sales, fundraising, advertising) and found thousands of tactics falling into six categories, each governed by one principle: consistency, reciprocation, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity.
- Material self-interest (getting the most for the least) is excluded on purpose, as a motivational given rather than a principle.

## Ch1 Weapons of influence

- Fixed-action patterns in animals are set off by a single trigger feature (the turkey mother responding to a chick's sound; robins attacking a tuft of red feathers). People have their own automatic, "click, whirr" responses.
- Langer's copier study: a request with a real reason got about 94% compliance vs about 60% with no reason; a reason that added nothing ("because I have to make some copies") got about 93%. The word "because" is a trigger.
- Turquoise jewelry that would not sell sold out when the price was accidentally doubled: expensive taken to mean good.
- Shortcuts are usually rational and necessary; the danger is exploiters who mimic the trigger. Influence works like jujitsu: the practitioner uses forces already present and appears to do nothing.
- Contrast principle: what we see second is judged against what we saw first. Clothing sellers sell the suit before the sweater; real-estate agents show setup houses; car dealers add options after the price is settled, each small against the big number.

## Ch2 Reciprocation

- The rule: repay in kind what another has given. Anthropologists describe it as universal and foundational to society.
- Regan study: a subject who received an unrequested soft drink from a confederate bought about twice as many raffle tickets from him; the favor overrode whether the subject liked him.
- The Hare Krishna flower technique: gift first, then the request; donations came even from people who threw the flower away.
- Uninvited debts: a charity mailing with individualized address labels nearly doubled its response (about 18% to 35%). Mauss: obligations to give, to receive and to repay. The obligation to receive is what makes the rule exploitable.
- Unequal exchanges: a small favor can draw a much larger return, because indebtedness is uncomfortable and people fear being seen as moochers.
- Reciprocal concessions: a Boy Scout's refused $5 ticket became an accepted $1 chocolate bar. The rejection-then-retreat technique: a large request refused, then a smaller one. In a study asking students to chaperone juvenile delinquents on a zoo trip, compliance went from about 17% to about 50% after a refused, much larger first request. A first ask too extreme to be seen as bargaining in good faith backfires.
- The technique combines with the contrast principle. The book's case: the Watergate break-in plan was approved after two far more expensive versions were rejected. Retail version: "talking the top of the line" (showing the most expensive model first) raised average sales.
- Side effects: people who agree after a retreat feel more responsible for the outcome and more satisfied, follow through more often, and agree to later requests more often.
- Endnotes: surprise requests do better; giving without letting the other person repay is also disliked across cultures; a free home inspection can be a setup for overpriced work.

## Ch3 Commitment and consistency

- After placing a bet, racetrack bettors feel more confident in their horse. In a beach study, few people stopped a staged theft of a stranger's radio, but nearly all did after agreeing to watch the stranger's things.
- Consistency is valued, and automatic consistency spares us from thinking (and from unwelcome conclusions).
- Toy-company tactic: advertise a toy before the holidays, undersupply it so parents buy substitutes, then advertise again after the holidays so parents keep their promise to the child.
- Commitment is the key: asking people to predict their own behavior raises the behavior. A charity caller who first asked how the person was feeling, and got a public "fine", raised agreement to a later request.
- Chinese handling of American prisoners in the Korean War: start small and build, from a mild statement that the U.S. is not perfect to written lists, signed statements, essays and broadcasts; the prisoners' self-image shifted.
- Foot-in-the-door technique: homeowners who first agreed to a small safe-driving sign were far more likely (about 76%) to accept a large, ugly billboard later than those asked cold.
- Commitments that work are active (written), public, effortful, and owned (the inner choice). Written: door-to-door companies have the customer fill in the agreement, which cuts cancellations. Public: public positions are held more stubbornly. Effortful: severe initiations raise how much members value a group. Inner choice: a large reward or threat prevents the person from owning the act; a barely sufficient reason works best (children told mildly not to play with a robot avoided it much longer than those threatened severely).
- Commitments "grow their own legs": people invent new reasons for a choice, so it stands after the original reason is removed. The lowball: car dealers offer a price or trade-in advantage, let the buyer commit, then remove it. In an Iowa energy study, residents promised publicity for conserving kept conserving, even more, after the publicity was withdrawn.
- Defenses: see `how-to-say-no.md`. Emerson: "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds."

## Ch4 Social proof

- Canned laughter makes people laugh longer, especially at weak material. We decide what is correct by what others do.
- Bartenders salt tip jars; evangelists plant ringers; advertisers claim to be fastest-growing. Modeling helps children overcome fear of dogs, especially with multiple models.
- A doomsday group, after the prophecy failed, began recruiting to gain social proof for its belief.
- Uncertainty: social proof is strongest when the situation is unclear. Pluralistic ignorance (everyone looks calm, so everyone concludes nothing is wrong) plus diffusion of responsibility explains bystander failures (Latane and Darley; the Kitty Genovese case). A lone bystander helps far more often than one in a group.
- Devictimizing yourself: in an emergency, reduce uncertainty and assign one person a task ("You, sir, in the blue jacket, I need help"). Cialdini used this after his own car accident.
- Similarity: people follow similar others. A lost-wallet study showed far more returns when the letter writer seemed similar. Peer-modeled programs work better.
- Werther effect: highly publicized suicides are followed by rises in suicides and in car and plane crashes among similar people. Jonestown: uncertainty after relocation plus a closed group of similar others turned a following into a herd.
- Defenses: counterfeit evidence (claques, canned laughter, staged testimonial ads presented as unrehearsed) and innocent herd errors. See `how-to-say-no.md`.

## Ch5 Liking

- Tupperware parties: the hostess is a friend and the real requester; the social bond drives purchases more than the product. Referral chains use a friend's name to open the next door. Joe Girard: a fair price from someone the customer likes.
- Physical attractiveness: a halo effect that affects voting, hiring and court outcomes, mostly without people noticing.
- Similarity: people help and buy from those like them, in dress, background, age and habits; trainers teach salespeople to "mirror and match". The book advises extra caution around requesters who seem just like you. Endnote: we underestimate how much similarity sways us.
- Compliments: Joe Girard sent every customer a card each month saying "I like you". Praise produced liking even when it was untrue and the flatterer wanted something.
- Contact and cooperation: familiarity breeds liking only when contact is positive; contact under competition breeds dislike (summer-camp studies; school desegregation). Shared goals reduce hostility (cooperative tasks; the jigsaw classroom). Salespeople manufacture cooperation, as in the car salesman's apparent battle with the boss on the buyer's behalf (an endnote describes it as typically staged) and good cop/bad cop.
- Conditioning and association: people dislike the bearer of bad news (weathermen; Persian messengers; a line from Shakespeare on bad news infecting the teller). Advertisers link products to attractive models, celebrities and good meals (the luncheon technique). Basking in reflected glory: fans say we after wins and they after losses, more so when their own image has just taken a hit.
- Defenses: watch for liking someone more than expected, then separate the requester from the request. See `how-to-say-no.md`.

## Ch6 Authority

- Milgram: about two thirds of ordinary adults kept delivering what they believed were dangerous shocks when instructed by an experimenter; when two authorities disagreed, or a peer gave the order, they stopped. The book reads this as a deep, trained duty to authority.
- Automatic deference has real costs: medication errors when staff follow a doctor's mistaken order (the rectal earache case). An actor who played a doctor on TV sold coffee on health grounds.
- Connotation, not content: titles (people estimate a higher-status person as taller), clothes (a man in a guard's uniform got far more compliance with an odd request than one in street clothes), and trappings (drivers honked less at a luxury car). In a hospital study, nearly all nurses were ready to give an over-limit dose of an unauthorized drug on a phone order from an unknown doctor; nurses asked to predict their behavior expected almost none would.
- People underestimate authority's pull on themselves.
- Defenses: two questions and a warning about a seller who seems to argue against their own interest (the waiter who steers you to a cheaper dish, then sells wine and dessert). See `how-to-say-no.md`.

## Ch7 Scarcity

- The scarcity principle: opportunities seem more valuable when they are less available. Loss framing beats gain framing (energy-saving and health-message studies).
- Limited-number and deadline tactics, true or false; can't-come-back offers designed to stop the buyer from thinking; boiler-room scripts that dangle and withdraw an opportunity.
- Psychological reactance: when free choice is limited or threatened, we want the thing more. Toddlers go for the toy behind a barrier; parental opposition intensifies teen romance (the Romeo and Juliet effect); a local ban on phosphate detergents made residents rate them better.
- Censorship raises both desire for and belief in the banned information. Jurors told to ignore inadmissible insurance information awarded more.
- Exclusive information persuades more. A beef importer's customers ordered about twice as much when told of a coming shortage, and about six times as much when told the news came from exclusive sources. An endnote says the information was true in every case, for ethical reasons.
- Optimal conditions: in a cookie study, cookies from a nearly empty jar were rated more desirable, especially if they had just become scarce, and most of all if they became scarce because others wanted them. Newly scarce beats always scarce; competition adds heat. Sellers fabricate rival buyers to push undecided customers ("goosing 'em off the fence"); auction bidding wars push prices past value.
- Defenses: treat rising arousal as a stop signal, then ask whether you want to have the thing or to use it. The book's line: "the scarce cookies didn't taste any better". An endnote stresses telling honest, naturally occurring scarcity from fabricated scarcity.

## Epilogue: Instant influence

- We use single-cue shortcuts more under time pressure, stress, uncertainty, indifference, distraction and fatigue, and modern life supplies all of these.
- Practitioners who use the principles honestly (a truthful claim to be the best seller) are allies. The enemies are those who "falsify, counterfeit, or misrepresent" the evidence our shortcuts rely on, and they deserve counterattack (refusing to buy, complaining).
