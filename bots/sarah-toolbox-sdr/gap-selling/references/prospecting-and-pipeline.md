# Gap Selling: Prospecting, Pipeline, Trials and Hiring (overflow from SKILL.md)

Added in the full-read revision. Quotes are verbatim from the book; everything else is paraphrase. The book's sample emails and Email Response Probability Scorecard were images and did not survive text extraction, so they are not reproduced here.

## You're not the customer's b*tch (Ch 12)

- Partner, not servant. "The customer is not always right." Watch ROSE (return on sales effort); "That often means learning to say no."
- **Servant tells:** they say jump and you ask how high; you send a quote without asking why; you give a free trial but they won't share results; you meet their requests but they won't meet yours; they like you only for pricing.
- **Asset tells:** you know their industry trends as well as they do, explain the nuances of their key problems, know the competition, bring continual insight, and they rely on you for direction.
- "If you find yourself afraid of losing the deal, it’s probably a sign you’ve lost control."
- **90-day trial demand.** Ask what it accomplishes. Either say no, or set terms: the customer pays an installation fee, provides clear and measurable success criteria, and commits to buy if the criteria are met. "I get why you want this trial, but just because you want it doesn’t mean I have to give it."
- **Pricing before discovery.** You can say no: you can't price until you understand their organization, problems and goals.

## More troubleshooting scripts (Ch 13)

- Criteria that don't serve the outcome: "I’m confused. You said growing revenue by increasing first-time ticket buyers was your most important priority, but some of the criteria you’ve said you’re going to use to decide whether to make this purchase won’t have any effect on increasing first-time ticket buyers."
- Price, second example: "How is $40,000 too much to pay for a service that will allow you to earn $50,000 more every month, indefinitely?"
- Budget: "Are you prepared to lose that kind of money, plus forgo competing for high-end projects for six more months?" "You’re merely forcing the buyer to defend his decision".
- Dark deal close-out questions: "Were you able to solve your problem? Have you decided not to pursue bigger projects?"
- Objections: "more often than not, the objection isn’t real". Hold it against the outcome (the theater's 1,500 subscribers vs a 50,000-ticket goal). If they correct your numbers, it's a real problem: solve it.

## Smart prospecting prep (Ch 15)

1. **Healthy pipeline:** "$1 million quota and you have a 30% closing percentage, then a healthy pipeline is roughly three million qualified opportunities." "The pipeline is the pathway to quota."
2. **Know who you're calling:** build an ICP from the PIC. Rank A (many problems, big impact), B (fewer problems, big impact), C (many problems, little impact), D (few problems, little impact; ignore). "Pain is everything."
3. **Be a problem finder.**
4. **Think like a buyer.** Keenan's "Dear Salesperson" letter: do you know my vendors, challenges and three-year plan? Are you a partner, vendor or supplier? "My upside is your upside. My downside is your downside." "Don’t say or send anything that wouldn’t compel you to pay attention if the roles were reversed."

## Capturing attention (Ch 16)

- "If you can’t generate intrigue, no one will pay attention to you."
- **Intrigue three ways:** surprise; mystery ("Did you know that your competitor shortened their manufacturing cycle by three days using our product?"); a knowledge gap ("Are you aware that many reliability managers are actually creating the very failures they are tasked with preventing?").
- **Educate with:** a competitor solving the problem, new regulation, new best practices, new markets, new data, unseen opportunities.
- **A clear, reasonable ask.** Ask small and secure the next yes.
- **Offer vs ask.** "Offer – Ask = Value". Net positive: 15 minutes on why their program costs 50% more than it should. Net negative: 30 minutes "to discuss their business and see if there are some areas where you can help". "It’s a giving email, not a taking email."

## Cadence (Ch 17)

- Every channel the buyer uses; social sellers beat quota more often in Keenan's research. Change the impact, information or research on every touch.
- **Book's schedule (business days):** email; +2 voicemail; +3 new email; +3-4 voicemail; a few days later email; then about every four days alternating call and email, "repeat up to 12 touches".
- From touch nine or ten with no response, a challenge email that puts the burden on the buyer. "Never say no for your buyer." "Don’t ever walk away until they tell you to go away."

## Managing the pipeline (Ch 18)

- InsideSales: "salespeople close three times as many deals at the end of the month as during the rest of the month—but lose 11 times as many."
- A review is the CRM Challenge run by a coach. Bad review: "Looks good. We’re waiting on the results of the demo and for the CIO to approve the solution." (a holding pattern; the deal's fate is in the buyer's hands). "If your salespeople cannot gain influence over their deals, they’re not selling; they’re pitching."
- Insist on specific data: "“The customer wants to grow faster” is not a fact." Make reps go back and get it.
- Review key deals only (biggest, nearest close). Watch average close rate, deal size, cycle length and new deals added.
- **The next yes:** "The next yes doesn’t come from your salespeople at all, but from their customers." Examples: intro to the CEO, a confidential attachment, written agreement on price, a site visit, a plant tour.
- **Stuck next yes:** get the blocker's own current state (IT won't open the API; security concerns).
- **Plan every yes ahead:** Keenan's ski-school platform stalled at IT, then HR killed it over wage-law risk. "You have to plan ahead for the next yes". New risk can flip the gap negative.
- A deal at the same next yes for weeks with no plan is effectively stalled.

## Commit culture (Ch 19)

- Commits land within 15% either way: a $500K commit can't come in at $400K or $580K.
- "Commit is an accuracy game, not a goal game." Never tell reps to raise a commit.
- Challenge, then accept: a $200K pipeline at a 40% close rate suggests about $80K. Ask "Can you tell me why you’re over-committing?" then accept their call.
- "More deals are lost, not because the customer didn’t have a problem or set of problems, but because the salesperson didn’t have enough information to effectively influence the deal to close."

## Hiring and leading (Ch 20-21)

- Nine traits: curiosity, critical thinking, empathy, problem solving, leadership, creativity, deliberate learning, coachability, business acumen (last on purpose; it can be learned).
- "53% of customer loyalty was not a product of customer service, as might be expected, but of the sales experience."
- Leaders coach; training fails without repetition and reinforcement in pipeline meetings and reviews.
- ARMS Reliability results after adopting gap selling: bookings up 38% in 12 months; conversion from 30% to 45%.
