## Rich Chiarello, Selling Technology the Sandler Way (Sandler Training, 2015)

**Ch 1: Fifty Years to Create the Problem.** Buyers of tech don't care about features ("end-user/feature disengagement"). Feature-led selling is "trying to sell from your knowledge base. It is death in today's market." Ask questions and diagnose, like a doctor.

**Ch 2: The Simple Math of Selling in the Tech Space.** Revenue = qualified-prospect revenue times close rate. Get twice as good at qualifying and 2 of 10 becomes 4 of 10. Call-back test: if you stopped calling, which qualified prospects would call you first? The ones who wouldn't don't need to buy as much as you need to sell.

**Ch 3: Product Knowledge: Use It or Lose It?** Product knowledge tempts sellers to sell too soon or let ego run the call. Buyers talk "70-80 percent" of the time. "It is much more important to understand why the prospect asked a certain question than it is to answer it." Qualified means at the point of sale on Pain, Budget and Decision, with business and personal pain.

**Ch 4: What Is Your Value Proposition?** "If everyone is a prospect, then no one is a prospect." The ideal prospect engages, shares urgency, signs off on mutual action plans and keeps talking. Hold back until it makes sense for the buyer to invest "money, time, attention and political capital". Chart: feature, business benefit, pain indicator (symptom), question to ask. Do your top three features. Best targets need what you do best and can't get elsewhere.

**Ch 5: Dealing with Prospecting Reluctance.** Head trash is "a problem that lies between the ears". The main cause: tying your identity to the prospecting role. The fix is an agreed cookbook of weekly and monthly calls, intro requests and new-department meetings. "You don't have to like prospecting. You just have to do it." Story: reps who only called DBAs because their contact said not to call other buyers; dozens of other vendors called those buyers anyway.

**Ch 6: The Stress-Free Sales Call.** Shift from selling to finding out if you should even talk. The three too's: talking too much, selling too soon, sounding too salesy. A hybrid call has a scripted first 90 seconds and the rest unscripted. Steps: pattern interrupt, 30-second commercial with three pain words, softening statement, UFC, fishing for pain, close for an hour-long meeting with other interested people, set homework, post-sell the appointment. "Our goal is to obtain information during the qualifying call, not provide it!" Scripts: `book-scripts.md` section 1.

**Ch 7: Diagnosis or Treatment?** Sales volume down is a symptom. "Prospects rarely bring you the real problem first." CRM example: reps not using CRM, with possible causes of UI, training, data entry, no win for reps, or feeling spied on. The size of the problem drives executive support. Treating symptoms is what peddlers do.

**Ch 8: Pain Is the Best Impending Event!** The buyer can always keep what they have. "You must find a big problem within a big account in order to extract big dollars." Without a real event, "don't forecast the sale!" Three kinds of pain: surface, business and personal. Discount deadlines are fake events and cause hockey-stick quarters. A paid trial on production data, with removal at the end, is a real event. Qualified means "have a problem you can solve, a budget with which you can work and a decision process they are willing to share with you." Bridge with "Is X something you would like to explore solving with us?" then launch the MAP. "Personal pain is the best impending event."

**Ch 9: Selling to the Stakeholders.** Four decisions: "1) technical, 2) business, 3) financial and 4) political." Corporate standards can overrule a business unit. A German division won locally, then HQ preferred its standard; the CIO said "we would not have to overrule your decision." Cast of characters: decision maker, user buyer, tech buyer, financial buyer, coach, key influencers. Mark each friend, neutral or enemy; "This can only be ascertained through a one-on-one conversation." "even one negative stakeholder can torpedo a deal." Read DISC from the words people use: active vs. reserved, task vs. people. Off the org chart: the CIO's outside advisor asked "Why switch?" and stalled the deal 60 days. Three outcomes, only one pays: you, a competitor, or no decision. Scripts: `book-scripts.md` section 5.

**Ch 10: All Prospects Are Not Created Equal.** Abundance is a belief, not a pipeline count. "People cannot force you to sell to them." Two criteria: the problem costs "at least three times what your fully implemented solution will cost", and the person has a personal stake and power, or will take you to who does. Budget is "a demonstrated willingness and ability to move money around to fund a desired business outcome." RFPs you didn't shape have poor odds. "RFP-like" buyers dictate every step. After Pain, Budget and Decision, build the MAP. It ends in "Sorry, but you're not a good fit," or "Send me a contract." Then use the biggest-fear post-sell. Scripts: `book-scripts.md` section 6.

**Ch 11: Handling Objections.** No objections halfway through a presentation usually means you're not a finalist. Three keys: "Handle your own anxiety. Understand the real objection. Defuse the prospect's emotion and put him back into a receptive mode." Defuse steps (paraphrase): listen all the way through; play it back and isolate one; agree with the feeling, not the objection; find the source; answer, then ask if it's addressed. Price is a game: "any price greater than zero places the prospect in the role of trying to get a lower price". If the buyer can't say what the objection costs them, someone else planted it. A real showstopper disqualifies. Scripts: `book-scripts.md` section 4.

**Ch 12: More on Mutual Action Plans.** Use the buyer's emotional peak to build a joint plan for "the last two-thirds of any sales cycle". The MAP tests whether the project is sanctioned or just one person's research. If the seller does all the tasks, it's "my deal", not "our deal". "If the prospect starts to balk at assuming any action items, you should take this as a big red flag that you have not uncovered enough personal pain." Seller tasks: interview stakeholders, demo, POC, proposal, references, post-sales intro. Buyer tasks: introduce stakeholders, give demo data, share ROI needs, run legal review, connect executives, raise internal visibility. Executive bridging lines up your managers with theirs. Volunteer as project manager. Story: a CIO's unsanctioned project wasted four months.

**Epilogue.** Recap of the book. No new method.

## Dave Mattson and Brian Sullivan, Sandler Enterprise Selling (McGraw-Hill, 2016)

**Read This First.** Enterprise selling vs. traditional selling is like chess vs. checkers. The submarine steps are Bonding & Rapport, Up-front Contract, Pain, Budget, Decision, Fulfillment and Post-sell. SES adds work before Bonding & Rapport and after Post-sell. There are six stages and 13 tools. The book is "a preview of the program, not a substitute for it."

**Pain (Ch 12) in more detail.** "Pain exists when there is a real or perceived gap between an existing situation and the desire for a better situation." Find "the problem, the underlying reasons, and the impact." "There is no way to win big revenue without identifying big problems." "People are willing to take action to resolve a problem situation only when they fully realize the depth of their pain."

**Decision (Ch 14) outcomes.** Fully qualified and fails: exit. Not finished: keep qualifying. Fully qualified and passes: go to Stage 4. You're disqualified if you can't solve the problem, the buyer can't or won't invest, or they "can't or won't share the process by which a decision will be made in a timely manner".

**Stage 6 numbers (as printed, no source given).** Existing customers bring 60-70% of business. Getting a new customer costs about seven times more than keeping one.

**Epilogue.** Recap of the six stages. "each enterprise client is a marketplace unto itself".
